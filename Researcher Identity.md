# Researcher Identity

> Fictional researcher profile for this workshop. The biography and research programme are illustrative; they do not describe the vault owner's identity or claim any completed research.

## Researcher

**Dr Rowan Ellis** is a fictional research fellow in the School of Computing at a fictional university. Rowan's background combines human–computer interaction, interaction design, and accessible mobile computing.

The first-person statements below express Rowan's proposed research position.

## Core identity

> **Accessible Spatial Interaction and Nonvisual Navigation**

## Distinctive intellectual theme

> **Communicating space through sound and touch**

I design and study nonvisual interfaces that help blind and low-vision people understand and navigate everyday spaces, with a focus on tactile interaction, audio guidance, and the demands of real environments.

## Core research problem

Navigation interfaces must communicate direction, distance, landmarks, and changes in a route while people are also attending to their surroundings. My research investigates how the amount, timing, and sensory form of that information affect both navigation and spatial understanding.

The central question is:

> **How can nonvisual interfaces communicate useful spatial information while leaving enough attention available for the surrounding environment?**

I am interested in the relationship between following an instruction, understanding a place, and recovering when a route or instruction becomes unclear. These are related outcomes that need to be examined separately.

## Research programme

The programme examines:

- how sound, speech, vibration, and tactile representations convey spatial information;
- how people interpret landmarks, junctions, distances, and route transitions through nonvisual interfaces;
- how guidance interacts with environmental sounds and existing mobility practices;
- how interfaces support orientation and recovery after missed turns or ambiguous cues;
- how sensory preferences, route familiarity, and environmental conditions affect interface use; and
- what makes a navigation interface useful during repeated everyday journeys.

Three connected streams structure [[10-Year Research Agenda]]:

1. Spatial information through sound and touch.
2. Navigation in everyday environments.
3. Personalisation and sustained use.

## Primary empirical context

The research centres on blind and low-vision adults navigating public buildings, transport interchanges, and the routes connecting them.

Candidate settings include unfamiliar entrances, complex indoor junctions, station concourses, and transitions between indoor and outdoor spaces. Candidate interfaces include mobile audio guidance, vibration cues, tactile route previews, and combinations of these approaches.

Participants' sensory experiences, mobility aids, route knowledge, and preferences are part of the research context. The programme should describe that variation rather than assume a single representative traveller.

## Methods

My intended methodological portfolio combines:

- participatory design with blind and low-vision people;
- development of interactive prototypes;
- controlled comparisons of spatial representations and cue designs;
- accompanied navigation studies in bounded settings;
- interviews and accessible diary studies about everyday journeys; and
- repeated-use field evaluations.

These are proposed approaches. Recruitment, partnerships, deployments, and findings have not been established for this fictional profile.

## Intended contribution

The programme aims to produce:

- accounts of how people interpret spatial information through sound and touch;
- empirical comparisons of cue timing, information density, and representation choices;
- interface designs for route previews, navigation, and recovery;
- evaluation methods that connect navigation outcomes with spatial understanding, attention, and participant experience; and
- design guidance for accessible navigation systems in public spaces.

Navigation time is one possible measure. Other outcomes include route comprehension, navigation errors, recovery, workload, detection of relevant environmental sounds, comfort, and willingness to use an interface again. The appropriate measures depend on the study question.

## Positioning and boundaries

The programme sits within human–computer interaction, accessibility, mobile interaction, and spatial cognition. Its computing contribution concerns the design, implementation, and evaluation of interactive representations of space.

Positioning technology and map data are enabling components. Work on their technical accuracy is central only when it explains an interface problem or supports an interaction contribution.

Adjacent topics include museum exploration, accessible tactile graphics, and navigation interfaces used when visual attention is occupied. They are useful when they illuminate spatial representation, sensory interaction, or orientation in the primary research context.

## Identity statements

### Short version

> I study how sound and touch can make spatial information and everyday navigation more accessible to blind and low-vision people.

### Full version

> My research examines how blind and low-vision people interpret and use spatial information through mobile, audio, and tactile interfaces. I investigate how interface design affects route comprehension, orientation, attention, and recovery in everyday environments, and develop designs that can accommodate different sensory preferences and mobility practices.

### Programme ambition

> I aim to build a sustained research programme connecting accessible interaction techniques with evidence from everyday journeys, producing reusable methods and design principles for nonvisual navigation.
