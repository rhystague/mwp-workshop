# Research Questions

> Question bank for Dr Rowan Ellis, the fictional workshop researcher. These are exploratory questions and prospective paper seeds, not established research gaps or findings.

Core identity:

> **Accessible Spatial Interaction and Nonvisual Navigation**

Distinctive intellectual theme:

> **Communicating space through sound and touch**

Current research context:

> Blind and low-vision adults using mobile, audio, and tactile interfaces to navigate public buildings, transport interchanges, and connecting routes.

Intended contribution:

> Understanding how the form and timing of spatial information affect route comprehension, orientation, attention, recovery, and sustained interface use.

Use these questions to guide reading and develop bounded studies. Claims of novelty or a literature gap require evidence from actual sources.

## Core questions

- How can nonvisual interfaces communicate useful spatial information while leaving enough attention available for the surrounding environment?
- What information helps a traveller understand the layout of a place as well as follow a route through it?
- How do interface requirements change between route preparation, active navigation, and recovery after a problem?
- How do sensory preferences, mobility practices, and environmental conditions shape the usefulness of different interface designs?

## Stream 1: Spatial information through sound and touch

- How do speech, non-speech audio, vibration, and combined cues affect comprehension of direction and distance?
- How much information can a cue convey before interpretation becomes slow or error-prone?
- Which representations help people distinguish a junction, a landmark, and a change of level?
- How do tactile route previews affect understanding of the relationships between places?
- When should an interface offer an overview of a route, and when should it provide local detail?
- How do cue timing and repetition affect comprehension during movement?
- What differences arise between recognising a cue in isolation and interpreting it during a navigation task?

## Stream 2: Navigation in everyday environments

- When do spoken instructions compete with environmental sounds that travellers need to hear?
- How do noise, interruptions, and crowding change the usefulness of audio and vibration cues?
- How can an interface communicate transitions between indoor and outdoor routes?
- Which interface features help people regain orientation after a missed turn?
- How should an interface indicate uncertain positioning or an ambiguous route instruction?
- How do people combine digital guidance with landmarks and established mobility practices?
- What differences appear between controlled navigation tasks and journeys in public spaces?

## Stream 3: Personalisation and sustained use

- Which interface preferences remain stable, and which change with route familiarity or environmental conditions?
- How much configuration do people want before a first journey, and what do they prefer to adjust while travelling?
- How should interfaces accommodate different combinations of residual vision, hearing, and tactile preferences?
- How do people change their use of route previews and guidance after repeated journeys?
- What leads participants to continue using, modify, or abandon a navigation interface?
- How do device placement, comfort, battery demands, and interaction effort affect everyday use?
- Can a small set of understandable settings accommodate useful variation without creating excessive setup work?

## Methods and evaluation

- How can navigation performance and spatial understanding be measured separately within the same study?
- Which tasks reveal whether a participant understands the layout of a place beyond the instructed route?
- How can studies observe attention to environmental sounds without treating route completion as sufficient evidence?
- How can participatory design sessions make audio, tactile, and spatial prototypes accessible to participants?
- How can route observations and accessible diaries explain differences between stated preferences and use?
- What can repeated-use studies reveal that a single-session comparison cannot?
- Which participant and setting characteristics need to be reported to make the limits of a study clear?

## Adjacent questions

These questions are adjacent unless they have a clear connection to nonvisual spatial representation or navigation for the programme's primary participants.

- How might nonvisual navigation techniques support open-ended exploration of museums or exhibitions?
- What can tactile diagram research contribute to the design of route previews?
- Which findings about navigation when visual attention is occupied can inform cue design, and where does that comparison break down?
- How do shared navigation experiences change the information an individual interface needs to provide?

## Paper seeds

These are candidate studies to assess against the literature and available methods.

- **Speech and vibration at complex junctions:** a controlled comparison of cue interpretation, navigation errors, and workload in a bounded route task.
- **Previewing a route through touch:** a design study of tactile previews, followed by an evaluation of layout comprehension and route recall.
- **Recovering after a missed turn:** an empirical study of orientation problems and interface responses during accompanied navigation.
- **Guidance amid environmental sound:** a study of instruction timing and information density, examining comprehension alongside detection of relevant sounds.
- **From first use to familiar journeys:** a repeated-use study of interface settings, route familiarity, comfort, and continued use.
- **Evaluating nonvisual navigation beyond completion time:** a methodological paper assessing a combination of performance, spatial understanding, and participant-experience measures.
