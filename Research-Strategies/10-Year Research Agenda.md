# 10-Year Research Agenda: Accessible Spatial Interaction and Nonvisual Navigation

> Illustrative agenda for Dr Rowan Ellis, the fictional workshop researcher. All studies, partnerships, funding directions, and outputs below are prospective. No completed work or established relationships are implied.

## Horizon

**2026–2035**

This is a living programme-level agenda for connecting research questions, prototypes, empirical studies, and longer-term contributions. Individual projects should change as evidence and practical opportunities develop.

## North star

> **How can nonvisual interfaces communicate useful spatial information while leaving enough attention available for the surrounding environment?**

The programme centres on blind and low-vision adults navigating public buildings, transport interchanges, and connecting routes. It examines the relationship between interface representations, environmental conditions, and the experience of a journey.

Following a route, understanding a place, and recovering after a navigation problem are distinct research outcomes. The programme will investigate how designs support each of them.

## Programme architecture

Three streams connect interaction techniques with everyday use:

1. **Spatial information through sound and touch** investigates representations of direction, distance, landmarks, and layout.
2. **Navigation in everyday environments** examines how those representations work during movement, interruptions, transitions, and recovery.
3. **Personalisation and sustained use** studies variation in preferences and mobility practices over repeated journeys.

The shared contribution is a body of design knowledge about accessible spatial interaction, supported by prototypes and evidence from both controlled and field settings.

## Stream 1: Spatial information through sound and touch

### Aim

Develop and evaluate ways to communicate spatial relationships through speech, non-speech audio, vibration, and tactile representations.

### Guiding questions

- How do different sensory representations affect comprehension of direction, distance, and layout?
- What information belongs in a route preview, a junction cue, or a local orientation request?
- How do cue timing, density, and repetition affect interpretation during movement?
- Which combinations of representations are useful, and under which task conditions?

### Candidate studies

- Participatory design of audio and tactile representations for a small set of navigation situations.
- Controlled comparisons of speech, vibration, and combined turn cues.
- Prototype studies of tactile route previews for unfamiliar public buildings.
- Experiments examining cue interpretation alongside spatial-relation and route-recall tasks.

### Intended longer-term contribution

- An empirically grounded design space for nonvisual spatial representations.
- Evidence about trade-offs among information detail, interpretation effort, and spatial comprehension.
- Reusable prototypes and clearly documented evaluation tasks.

## Stream 2: Navigation in everyday environments

### Aim

Explain how nonvisual guidance works when travellers encounter environmental sounds, interruptions, route transitions, and navigation problems.

### Guiding questions

- How do noise and interruptions affect the interpretation of guidance?
- How should interfaces support indoor–outdoor transitions and complex junctions?
- What information helps a person regain orientation after a missed turn or ambiguous instruction?
- How do interface cues fit with landmarks and participants' existing mobility practices?

### Candidate studies

- Accompanied navigation studies in bounded public-building and interchange settings.
- Comparisons of instruction timing and detail under different acoustic conditions.
- Controlled simulations of missed-turn and positioning-uncertainty scenarios before field evaluation.
- Interview and observation studies examining how participants combine interface information with environmental cues.

### Intended longer-term contribution

- Explanations of where controlled interaction results transfer to everyday navigation and where they do not.
- Designs for communicating route transitions, ambiguity, and recovery information.
- Field-study methods that connect observed navigation episodes with participants' interpretations.

## Stream 3: Personalisation and sustained use

### Aim

Understand how accessible navigation interfaces can accommodate different sensory preferences and mobility practices over repeated journeys.

### Guiding questions

- Which preferences are stable, and which depend on the journey or environment?
- How does route familiarity change the information people request?
- What configuration and adjustment mechanisms are useful during everyday travel?
- What explains continued use, modification, or abandonment of an interface?

### Candidate studies

- Participatory design of a small set of understandable interface settings.
- Repeated-route studies comparing initial use with later journeys.
- Accessible diary and interview studies of comfort, interruptions, adjustment, and practical burden.
- Longer field deployments once prototype reliability and appropriate study arrangements are established.

### Intended longer-term contribution

- An account of how interface requirements vary across people, places, and time.
- Evidence-based approaches to configuration and in-journey adjustment.
- Design guidance that considers sustained usefulness as well as initial usability.

## Cross-stream research design

### Forms of evidence

Each study should choose measures that answer its specific question. Candidate evidence includes:

- cue interpretation accuracy and response time;
- route completion, navigation errors, stops, and recovery episodes;
- tasks examining landmark relationships, layout comprehension, and route recall;
- detection of relevant environmental sounds under defined study conditions;
- participant reports of workload, comfort, and orientation;
- observations of interface use and changes to settings; and
- accessible diaries and interviews about repeated journeys.

A faster journey alone does not establish better spatial understanding or a more suitable interface. Laboratory measures of sound detection also need explicit limits when interpreting their relevance to travel in public spaces.

### Methodological portfolio

- Participatory design to develop research priorities and accessible prototypes.
- Controlled studies to isolate representation and timing choices.
- Accompanied navigation to examine interaction within a route and setting.
- Mixed-method studies connecting observations, task outcomes, and participant accounts.
- Repeated-use studies to examine familiarity and practical fit.
- Literature synthesis to identify what is established, contested, or insufficiently examined.

Recruitment and reporting should account for differences in sensory experience, mobility practices, and route familiarity. Work with blind and low-vision participants is central; results from sighted participants performing simulated nonvisual tasks should not stand in for their experience.

## Ten-year development pathway

### Phase 1 — Establish questions and prototypes (2026–2028)

Priorities:

- map the literature on nonvisual spatial representations and navigation interfaces;
- seek participant and community input to refine the initial research questions;
- develop a small set of audio, vibration, and tactile prototypes;
- establish accessible study materials and bounded navigation tasks; and
- conduct initial studies linking cue design with comprehension and navigation outcomes.

Evidence of progress would include a defensible account of the research problem, feasible prototypes, and early findings with clearly stated limits. Literature gaps should be demonstrated before being used to justify individual papers.

### Phase 2 — Connect techniques with everyday settings (2029–2031)

Priorities:

- extend promising designs into public-building and interchange contexts;
- study route transitions, environmental sounds, and recovery;
- compare controlled results with observations from accompanied journeys;
- begin repeated-use studies of configuration and route familiarity; and
- develop linked doctoral projects across the three streams.

Evidence of progress would include explanations of context-dependent results and interface revisions grounded in participant experience. Potential deployment partners would be approached as the requirements become clear.

### Phase 3 — Consolidate and examine transfer (2032–2035)

Priorities:

- integrate findings about representation, environment, and personalisation;
- evaluate sustained use across a broader range of routes and settings;
- examine which design principles transfer and which remain context-specific;
- publish reusable prototypes, study materials, and suitably shareable evidence; and
- translate supported findings into guidance for accessible navigation interface design.

Evidence of progress would include connected findings across studies, transparent evaluation resources, and guidance whose applicability and limitations are documented.

## Research student project directions

Possible doctoral projects include:

- tactile route previews and the interpretation of unfamiliar layouts;
- audio guidance and attention to environmental sounds;
- interface support for orientation and recovery after navigation problems; and
- personalisation and sustained use across repeated journeys.

Smaller projects could examine a bounded cue set, prototype interaction, or evaluation task. Each project should have a distinct question while contributing to the shared research programme.

## Publication and funding logic

The planned publication portfolio connects four contribution types:

1. **Conceptual and synthesis:** organising spatial representations, navigation situations, and evaluation outcomes.
2. **Technical and design:** implementing and examining audio, tactile, and mobile interaction techniques.
3. **Empirical:** explaining interface use across controlled tasks and everyday journeys.
4. **Methodological:** developing accessible study procedures and evaluating measures of navigation and spatial understanding.

Potential funding proposals would address specific access problems, such as navigating unfamiliar public buildings or recovering from ambiguous route guidance. Partner roles, technical feasibility, and evidence needs would be established for each proposal rather than assumed in advance.

## Strategic guardrails

- Keep accessible spatial interaction and nonvisual navigation at the centre.
- Connect technical components to a clear interaction question.
- Specify the participants, routes, environments, and interface conditions to which findings apply.
- Treat differences between participants as part of the research problem.
- Evaluate route performance alongside the outcomes relevant to the particular study, such as comprehension, attention, recovery, or comfort.
- Move from bounded prototypes to field studies as the evidence and practical arrangements support it.
- Use adjacent settings selectively to examine the transfer of a specific concept or design.
- Keep prospective claims separate from findings established through actual research.

## Near-term priorities: next 18–24 months

1. Conduct a focused literature review on audio and tactile representations for nonvisual navigation.
2. Refine a bounded initial question with input from blind and low-vision participants.
3. Build comparable prototypes for spoken, vibration, and combined junction cues.
4. Design an initial study of cue interpretation, route comprehension, and workload.
5. Scope a subsequent study of orientation and recovery in a public-building setting.
6. Identify practical requirements for accessible recruitment, study materials, and accompanied journeys.
