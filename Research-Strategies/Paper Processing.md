Do not keep one giant list. That makes every paper feel equally urgent.

| **Wildcards**         | Keeps it fun                        | papers that feel exciting, strange, provocative, or adjacent                                                            |
The rule: **Core Identity gets priority; Wildcards keep you interested.**

## 2. Use a three-pass reading method

Most papers do not deserve a full read immediately. Use a fast triage method.

### Pass 1: The 7-minute scan

Read only:

1. title
    
2. abstract
    
3. introduction final paragraph
    
4. research questions
    
5. methods summary
    
6. findings/contribution
    
7. conclusion
    

Then decide:

|Decision|Meaning|
|---|---|
|**Keep**|This directly helps your research identity|
|**Skim later**|Useful, but not urgent|
|**Cite only**|Good background, but not worth deep reading|
|**Discard**|Interesting, but not your lane|

Your goal is to reduce the backlog, not conquer it.

### Pass 2: The 25-minute useful read

For papers that survive Pass 1, read with one question:

> “What can I use this for?”

Capture only:

- the paper’s main claim
    
- the concept or framework it gives you
    
- the method used
    
- one useful quote or paraphrased idea
    
- how it connects to your research identity
    

### Pass 3: The deep read

Reserve deep reading for papers that do one of these:

- define a field
    
- give you a theory/framework
    
- directly support a paper you are writing
    
- challenge your thinking
    
- could become a “spine citation” in your work
    

This keeps deep reading special rather than exhausting.

## 5. Create “map notes”, not endless paper notes

The real value is not one note per paper. The value is in synthesis notes.

Create Obsidian notes like:

- `Nonvisual navigation`
    
- `Route following and spatial comprehension`
    
- `Audio and tactile interfaces`
    
- `Navigation in everyday environments`
    
- `Evaluating nonvisual navigation`
    
- `Indoor–outdoor route transitions`
    
- `Personalisation and sustained use`
    
- `Orientation and recovery after navigation errors`
    

Each time you read a paper, add one or two insights to the relevant map note.

This is how the knowledge becomes usable. You are not just collecting articles; you are building your conceptual territory.

The flow should be:

Read paper
   ↓
Create tiny paper note (just for capture of where you can use it)
   ↓
Move 1–3 ideas into synthesis notes
   ↓
Link back to paper note/Zotero
   ↓
Archive the paper note unless needed

## 6. Make reading fun with “research quests”

Instead of saying “I need to read 20 papers”, set small quests.

Examples:

### Quest 1: Find the field boundary

> How do route guidance, orientation, and spatial understanding differ in nonvisual navigation?

Read 5–8 papers. Capture the distinction.

### Quest 2: Find your argument

> What does the literature establish about nonvisual navigation, and which questions remain unresolved?

Read 5 papers and write a 300-word position.

### Quest 3: Find your methods

> How are people studying audio and tactile navigation interfaces during everyday journeys?

Read 5 methods sections only.

### Quest 4: Find the everyday navigation link

> How do interface findings from controlled navigation tasks relate to journeys in public spaces?

Read across accessible interaction design, spatial cognition, and navigation field studies.

This turns reading into exploration rather than homework.

The Friday paragraph is critical. It converts reading into identity-building.

Example (fictional; illustrates the format rather than reporting findings):


## 10. Use a “read, react, reuse” rule

For each paper, you only need three outputs:

2. **React:** What do I think about it?

This protects your time.

## 12. Use AI carefully as a reading companion

You can use AI to reduce friction, but not to replace engagement.

Good uses:

- ask for a plain-English explanation of a difficult section
    
- compare two papers
    
- extract the research question, method, and findings
    
- <mark style="background: #FFFF00;">generate questions to ask while reading</mark> (this makes it a game)
    
- turn highlights into a structured note
    
- identify possible gaps or tensions
    

Poor uses:

- asking AI to summarise everything so you never read
    
- collecting summaries without thinking
    
- trusting claims without checking the paper
    
- letting the tool decide what matters
    

A good prompt for your workflow:

```text
I am reading this paper as part of my research identity: Accessible Spatial Interaction and Nonvisual Navigation.

Help me extract:
1. the central claim
2. the contribution
3. the method
4. the assumptions
5. what this adds to my field map
6. what I should be sceptical about
7. where I could use this in a future paper
```
